Antiquity · Stoicism · c. 170 CE
The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way.
Med.
V.20
Meditations, Book V, §20
Marcus Aurelius · written c. 170–180 CE · translated by George Long, 1862

Why this matters

Aurelius is writing to himself during a plague and a war. He is not saying obstacles are good — he is saying that the mind's response to an obstacle is itself a kind of progress. The Stoic move is to treat the interruption not as a break from the work but as a new part of it.

This single sentence became one of the most quoted in Stoicism, and re-entered wide circulation through Ryan Holiday's 2014 book of the same title. But the thought is older than Aurelius; it echoes Epictetus's insistence that what happens to us is indifferent, and only our use of it matters.

From the source — Meditations, V.20 (Long translation, public domain)

"In a sense, people are our proper occupation. Our job is to do them good and put up with them. But when they obstruct our proper tasks, they become irrelevant to us — like sun, wind, animals. Our actions may be impeded by them, but there can be no impeding our intentions or our dispositions. Because we can accommodate and adapt. The mind adapts and converts to its own purposes the obstacle to our acting. The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."

Connections

The idea threads forward to Seneca's insistence that hardship is the proving ground of virtue, and sideways to Epictetus's dichotomy of control — that freedom begins at the line between what is and isn't up to us. It reappears, secularized, in William James's account of habit and, later still, in the American tradition of self-reliance.
